A Phone for People Who Read the Spec Sheet

R&D plan for our class project: an Android phone for technical buyers that feels engineered, costs around $500, and is honest about every part inside it.

The Short Version

At this price, no one designs their own processors, screens, or camera sensors. Google, Nothing, and Xiaomi all buy them from the same handful of suppliers. What separates one $500 phone from another is which parts get picked, how the body is built, and what the software lets the owner do. That is where our design effort goes.

The biggest constraint right now is memory. RAM and storage prices roughly quadrupled over the past year, and memory now takes about 40% of a midrange phone’s parts budget. Most of the decisions below follow from that.

Target price
$549 for 8 GB and 128 GB. A $499 version is possible with the fallback chip.
Chip platform
MediaTek Dimensity 8500. Qualcomm Snapdragon 7 Gen 4 if we hold $499.
Memory
8 GB RAM, 128 GB storage, and a microSD slot.
What we design
Machined aluminum frame, front home button with fingerprint sensor, alert slider, headphone jack with its own DAC, screwed-together repairable build, and an open bootloader.
How it gets built
Joint development with an experienced phone ODM. We own the design; they bring board engineering, certification help, and the factory.

Who It’s For

From our board: a technical US buyer, young adult to middle-aged, middle class to high end, value-driven, who cares how things are made. They read reviews, compare benchmark scores, and notice when a button feels cheap. The iPhone’s design aims at a different crowd, which leaves room for a phone that looks and feels like a precision tool.

That buyer gives us three rules for every decision:

  • Every claim on the box should be something a reviewer can measure and confirm.
  • Controls used without looking (unlock, silence, audio) should be physical.
  • The owner should be able to open the phone, replace the battery, and install their own software.

What This Tells Us

  • Both phones Americans can buy new at $499 ship 8 GB of RAM and sit in the same performance tier. Reviewers already call the Pixel 10a’s 8 GB tight.
  • Raw speed per dollar belongs to Xiaomi’s Poco line, with a chip about 50% faster, but it isn’t sold officially in the US.
  • Repairability belongs to Fairphone, at $649 with a slower chip.
  • Neither the Pixel 10a nor the Nothing (4a) Pro has a headphone jack. Only about 15–20% of phone models still had one in 2025, mostly gaming phones and budget Motorolas.
  • No one sells Poco-class speed, a real headphone jack, and Fairphone-style repair together for around $500. That gap is our product.

The Budget Problem Is Memory

AI data centers are buying up the world’s memory supply, and phones are paying for it. A few numbers from 2026:

  • Phone RAM went from about $2.80 per GB in 2025 to about $12 in 2026, based on Morgan Stanley data cited by Google.
  • TrendForce measured LPDDR5X contract prices rising 78–83% in a single quarter (Q2 2026).
  • Counterpoint estimates memory is now about 40% of a midrange phone’s parts cost, and DRAM has passed the processor as the most expensive single part in premium phones.
  • Phone makers are settling on 8 GB as the standard for midrange models, according to TrendForce.
  • Even Apple felt it: Counterpoint puts the iPhone 17e’s parts cost 15.6% above the iPhone 16e’s, mostly from memory.

Our response is 8 GB of RAM, 128 GB of storage, and a microSD slot. The slot lets us buy less flash at today’s prices and lets owners add cheap storage themselves, which this buyer will appreciate.

How the Model Works

Memory uses $8–12 per GB for LPDDR5X RAM and $0.25–0.50 per GB for UFS flash, which brackets reported 2026 contract prices. Chip makers don’t publish prices, so the chip ranges are our estimates. The other parts are scaled from TechInsights’ Pixel 9a teardown, where the processor was about 30% of the parts cost, the cameras about 15%, and the display about 7%.

The implied price assumes parts make up 65% of the selling price, roughly what a lean brand selling direct online could run. Large brands sit far lower: Counterpoint’s estimate put the iPhone 15 Pro Max’s parts at about 42% of its price.

Where the Rest of the Money Goes

The other 35% has to cover 5G patent licenses (Qualcomm and other patent holders each charge a share of the phone’s price), certification and tooling spread across every unit, warranty and returns, shipping and payment fees, marketing, and whatever is left as profit. That is why $549 is a more honest target than $499, unless we sell direct and accept a very thin margin.

Choosing the Chip Platform

The chip platform is the first hardware decision because it fixes most of the others. It comes with the 5G modem, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, GPS, the camera image processor, a reference board design, and the Android support package. It also limits which manufacturers can build the phone, since each ODM works with certain platforms.

One correction to our block diagram: it shows GPS, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth as separate modules. In a current phone those come from the platform (on the main chip or its companion chips), so we don’t source them separately.

Our Pick: MediaTek Dimensity 8500

  • About 50% faster than the Pixel 10a and Nothing (4a) Pro in AnTuTu, which gives our performance claim something real to stand on.
  • New for 2026, built on TSMC’s efficient 4 nm process with all big CPU cores.
  • Already shipping in phones that sell for about $355–420, so the cost fits our class.
  • MediaTek has long worked with ODMs on ready-made phone designs, which suits a small brand.

The risks are a smaller custom ROM community than Snapdragon’s and games that are sometimes tuned for Qualcomm graphics first. We answer the first by publishing kernel source and device files on launch day, and the second by testing popular games on early prototypes.

Fallback: Qualcomm Snapdragon 7 Gen 4

If $499 is a hard line, this is the chip. It matches Nothing’s, brings Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6.0, and has Qualcomm’s stronger custom ROM community. We would then compete on build, audio, and openness rather than speed. The Snapdragon 8s Gen 4 is slightly faster than the Dimensity 8500 but pushes the price toward $590 in our model.

What We Design Ourselves

These are the parts of the phone that carry our name. Each one has an engineering cost, listed with it.

Machined Aluminum Frame

The first thing the buyer touches, so it carries the engineered feeling: cool metal, crisp chamfers, tight seams.

The catch: metal blocks radio signals. The frame needs antenna breaks (thin plastic-filled slots) placed with the ODM’s radio team, and the back has to be glass if we want wireless charging.

Home Button with Fingerprint Sensor

Unlock and go home without looking, with a real click.

The catch: the button takes the bottom of the front face, so the phone gets a little taller or the screen a little smaller. We settle the size early with physical models.

Alert Slider

A three-position switch for ring, vibrate, and silent.

The catch: one more opening to seal and one more mechanical part to test for thousands of cycles.

Headphone Jack with Its Own DAC

Wired audio through a proper DAC (digital-to-analog converter, the chip that turns digital audio into the signal headphones play) and amplifier.

The catch: the jack takes board and frame space and is another path for water, so we target IP65 rather than IP68 unless the ODM has a proven sealed jack.

Repairable Build

Screws instead of glue on the back, a pull-tab battery, and published spare part prices. Fairphone sells a replacement screen for $90 and a battery for $40, which sets the bar.

The catch: screws and gaskets add a little thickness and assembly time.

Sustained Performance

Engineers notice when a phone slows down after a few minutes of load. A vapor chamber keeps the chip near full speed, and we publish our own sustained-load results.

The catch: it adds cost, thickness, and weight.

Open Software

Samsung removed bootloader unlocking from all its phones with One UI 8, and Xiaomi has restricted it too. Supporting unlocking and relocking, and publishing kernel source and device files at launch, costs little and matters a lot to this buyer.

The catch: unlocked phones can fail some banking and payment app checks, so relocking has to be easy.

From Idea to Factory

Who Builds It

Almost no phone brand builds every phone itself. Three kinds of partner do the work:

ODM (original design manufacturer)
Designs and builds the phone for a brand. Huaqin, Longcheer, and Wingtech shipped more than three quarters of all outsourced phones in 2023.
IDH (independent design house)
Designs the electronics; a separate factory builds them.
EMS (electronics manufacturing services)
Builds to the brand’s own design. Apple and Google design in-house but outsource all manufacturing this way.

Motorola and Xiaomi outsource most of their phone designs (Xiaomi about 78% in early 2024), and even Samsung uses ODMs for some Galaxy A models. We would work as a joint development client: we own industrial design, mechanics, the differentiating features, and software direction. The ODM brings the chip platform, board and radio engineering, certification support, and the factory.

Where the phone is assembled also matters. US tariff rules shifted several times in 2026. Trade trackers show smartphones exempted from the broad new import duties, but this changes quickly, so we check again before choosing an assembly country.

Open Questions for the Team

  • Is $549 acceptable, or is $499 a hard line? That decides between the Dimensity 8500 and the Snapdragon 7 Gen 4.
  • Glass back with wireless charging, or a full metal back without it? Metal feels more machined but loses wireless charging and makes antennas harder.
  • How much screen are we willing to give up for the front home button?
  • IP65 or IP68? The jack and slider make IP68 harder and more expensive.
  • Should we build a physical model? A 3D-printed or machined shell with a working button and slider would sell the tactile idea better than any render.
  • What do we call it?
